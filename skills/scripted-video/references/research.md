# Research

Two stages: first learn **how to research this subject**, then research it in **three planned
rounds**: the outline, the detail, and a double check of everything the video will say. Skip
both only when the content is fully given and you add no facts of your own.

This file is the method behind the three principles at the top of `SKILL.md`: research **one
level deeper than the video tells** (to the depth of a **detail**, the level a longer version
would tell), and no deeper; **only examples that show the main topic at work**; and research with
**an edge and an end**: every lead tested against the main topic, three planned rounds, the last
one a double check that widens nothing. The extra level chooses the core and finds the next
video; below it lie each person's later life, each document in full and an institution's whole
history, which are the research of another video. A video with fewer side stories and every fact
checked twice is better than one with every side story and facts read once.

Everything goes into `notes/<id>/research/` as markdown. The notes are the video's memory: the
mind map points into them, the accuracy review checks the script against them, and the sources
list in the publishing kit is copied from them. Write them so that someone who has not seen your
searches can use them.

## 1. Stage 1: how to research this subject

A beginner searching a subject directly finds the most repeated explanation, not the best one, and
cannot tell which details matter. So spend the first searches on the **method**, at two levels:

| Level | Example for "how the bond market works" | What it gives you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The subject | "how to understand the bond market", "how do analysts read bond yields", "bond market for beginners common mistakes" | The questions experts ask, the numbers they watch, the standard teaching order. |
| The field above it | "how to learn about the economy", "how economists explain interest rates" | The ideas the subject rests on, which the viewer will also need. |

For each level, find out:

- **How people who know this field find things out.** What do they measure or read? What counts
  as evidence (an experiment, a data series, a primary document, a court ruling, a standard)?
- **Where the trustworthy material is.** Which institutions publish the data, which textbooks or
  reference works are standard, which bodies set the definitions. For a historic event: was
  there an official inquiry, a court case, a published archive, a memoir by someone who was in
  the room?
- **How it is usually taught, and in what order.** The usual first example is usually a good one.
- **Where beginners go wrong.** Search "common misconceptions about …" and "what people get wrong
  about …". These become the video's warnings, and the things your own script must not say.
- **Where the experts disagree,** or where the popular account differs from the careful one.

Write the result in `00-how-to-research.md`: the searches you ran, what you learned about method,
and a short **research plan** (the notes you intend to write and what each must answer). Keep it
to a page. Its purpose is to change what you search for next.

## 2. Stage 2: what to collect

| Collect | Why the video needs it |
| --- | --- |
| **Facts and definitions**, each with a source | So the script says only what is supported. |
| **Numbers**, with unit, date, who measured them and **what exactly they measure** | A number without a date goes stale; without a unit it misleads; without its measure it gets compared with the wrong thing (section 5). |
| **Real examples and cases**, each known to the depth of a detail: dates, times, places, names, roles, amounts, the exact means, what was said, what came after | An idea is believed when the viewer sees it happen once. Invented people (a farmer, a customer) explain the mechanism; a real event proves it exists. A thin real example ("in 1992 a trader made a billion") teaches nothing: the detail is the lesson. See section 4. |
| **What the requester said or remembers** about the subject | It tells you what they want in the video. It is also a claim like any other; see section 8. |
| **Everyday comparisons** (an analogy from daily life), and where each one breaks down | An analogy carries a new idea on an old one. Every analogy is wrong somewhere; know where. |
| **Other views, objections and counter-examples** | See section 8. |
| **What the viewer probably believes already**, right or wrong | The video starts there. |
| **Words of the field** and their plain-language meaning, including every term a source uses that a beginner would not know | Candidates for word cards; also words to avoid. A detail can only go into the video if its words can be explained. |
| **Exact quotations**, with who said it, when, and where it is printed | A real voice in the story. Short, exact, attributed. |
| **Pictures that exist:** the standard diagram, a famous chart, the data behind a chart, what the thing looks like | The storyboard will need them, and a standard diagram is standard for a reason. |
| **Leads**: everything the material mentions and does not explain | Tested against the main topic; those that pass are the next round (section 3). |

## 3. Research in rounds: the leads ledger

One pass of searching gives the summary everyone repeats. The detail, and most of the
corrections to the summary, sit one or two questions further in. So research runs in **rounds**,
and a ledger (`research/leads.md`) records every question the material raised and what was done
with it.

**A lead is anything a source mentions that your notes do not yet explain.** Read each note you
have just written, sentence by sentence, and list:

- every **person** (who were they, what was their job on that day, what did they do);
- every **organisation, place, document, law, rule or product** named;
- every **date and time** (what happened just before and after);
- every **number** (who counted it, what it measures, what other figures exist);
- every **term** a beginner would not know;
- every **mechanism** stated without its steps ("the bank defended the price": how, exactly?);
- every **cause** stated without its reason, and every "so", "because", "as a result";
- every **quotation** not yet traced to where it was first printed;
- every **disagreement** between two sources;
- every question a curious viewer would ask next: "and then what?", "why did nobody stop it?",
  "who paid?", "is that a lot?".

### The test: does this lead bear on the main topic?

The **main topic** is the promise in the brief ("after watching, the viewer can …") and the
real stories chosen to show it. Write it at the top of the ledger in one sentence. Every lead is
tested against that sentence **before** any search is spent on it:

> If the answer came back, could I write a sentence with it that helps this viewer understand
> the main topic, or one of its stories as an example of the main topic?

| The lead | Verdict | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How exactly the thing was done in this event; each step of the mechanism | On topic | It is the subject, happening. |
| Who did what in the event, and in what job | On topic | The story cannot be told without it. |
| Each number that gives the size of what happened; who counted it | On topic | |
| Why it turned out as it did; the warnings; what was found afterwards; what is disputed | On topic | |
| What came of it for the people and bodies in the story | On topic, briefly | One or two facts each: the outcome, not the later career. |
| A person's life before the event | On topic only if it explains what they did in it | A trader's training, yes. Their school, no. |
| The history of an institution the story passes through | On topic only as far as the event needs | "The bank was 230 years old" is the story. Its nineteenth-century loans are another video: a next-video row if they make a story. |
| Each warning, document, hearing or court case in full; each person's later life | Below the depth of a detail | Not researched. The detail says there were ten warnings and what was done; the words of each are another video. |
| Another event a source mentions in passing | Off topic, unless the main topic is at work in it (the example test) | If it is, research it to the depth the core needs (what happened, when, who, what it shows about the main topic) and make it a next-video row. |
| The detail of a neighbouring field (the law of the case, the politics of the day) | Off topic beyond what a line of the video needs | |
| A lead met while researching a lead | Tested against **the main topic**, not against the lead that raised it | This is where research drifts: each step is "related" to the one before, and three steps on, the subject is out of sight. |

When you are unsure, ask what the video would lose without the answer. If a line of the video
would be wrong or unexplained: on topic. If the video would only be shorter by a curiosity:
off topic.

A lead that fails the test is written in the ledger as **off topic**, with the reason in a few
words, and **not researched**. It is not lost: the list of off-topic leads goes into the
hand-over, and the requester can ask for any of them.

### The example test: is the main topic at work in it?

Before a real event is researched in depth or planned into the video, ask of it:

> Does the thing this video is about actually happen in this story, as the sources tell it?

For a video about futures markets: were futures contracts traded on an exchange, was margin
called, was a contract settled? Not "was money bet on a price", not "were futures markets busy
that day", not "it is often described as a futures story". Answer from the sources, in round 1,
and write the answer, with its source, in the note's "The link to the subject" (section 4).

- **Yes:** the event can be one of the video's real examples.
- **No, or only at the edges:** the event is not an example for this video, however famous, and
  even if the requester suggested it. It is not told in the video. If it has a story of its own,
  it is a row of `next-videos.md`; tell the requester what the sources show and why it is out.

On one job, a fund's famous bet against a currency in 1992 was offered as the example for a
video about futures. The sources showed the bet was made through private deals with banks, not
on a futures exchange; futures only appeared at the edges (the same fund held other futures, and
an exchange had a busy day). Two editions told it at length, with a line saying it was not a
futures bet, before it was taken out. The test above, done in round 1, would have saved that.
In the same job, a bank that fell through its trader's futures bets passed the test easily: the
contracts, the margin calls and the exchange are the story.

### Neighbouring subjects: the next video

Some leads are more than off topic: they are a subject in their own right, with people, a
timeline and an ending, and they touch the main topic. In one job, the research into futures
trading turned up a bank that fell through futures bets. Its fall shows the main topic at work,
so the core told it: what happened, when, who, and what it shows about futures. Its two
centuries of history, the inquiries in full and the court cases are a video of their own. A
subject that fails the example test can still be a row here; it is just not told in this
video.

For each such subject, write a row in `research/next-videos.md`:

| Column | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| The subject | One line: what and when. |
| How it touches this video's topic | The link, in a sentence. Say whether the main topic is at work in it (the example test). |
| In this video | The core's lines about it (chapter, first words), or "not in the video". |
| Already known | Pointers into the notes (`note.md#Heading`); what is confirmed. |
| A video of its own would need | The questions still open, the main sources found, roughly how many rounds. |
| Worth it? | Your view, in a line: is there a story, a promise for a viewer, enough sources? |

Do not research it beyond what the core needs. The table is the hand-over's list of next
videos, and a later job starts its research from it.

### Three rounds, each with one job

| Round | Its job | Ends when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. The outline** | The notes from the research plan: the standard account, the real events chosen, the main sources found and opened. List the leads; test each. | Every note in the plan exists and the ledger is filled in. |
| **2. The detail** | Research every on-topic lead from round 1, to the depth of a detail. The answers raise leads of their own: test them, and research those that pass **once**, in this round. Neighbouring subjects go to `next-videos.md`. | Every on-topic lead is researched or "not found". |
| **3. The double check** | No new subjects. Read again, at the source, everything the video is going to say (next heading). | Every claim headed for the video has been checked and labelled. |

A **fourth round** is allowed for one purpose: items the double check could not settle and that
the **core** of the video needs (a contradiction between two sources, a figure nobody can be
shown to have counted, a fact the requester remembers that is still unverified). It is limited
to those items, listed by name before it starts. **There is no fifth round** unless the
requester asks for one. What is still open after the last round stays out of the video and is
listed in the hand-over with what would close it.

Before each round, decide what it may cost: how many searches and pages (section 9, "Plan the
searches"). A round ends when its list is done or its allowance is spent, whichever comes first.

Count the leads in each round and write the counts in `research/index.md`. They should fall
steeply: for one historic event, something like 30 on topic in round 1, 10 new in round 2, none
researched in round 3. **If round 2 raises more on-topic leads than round 1 did, the test is too
loose or the main topic is too wide.** Stop, read the off-topic examples above again, and narrow
it; do not add rounds.

### How a lead closes

| Status | Meaning | Must say |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **researched** | The answer is in the notes. | Which note and heading. |
| **not found** | You looked and the sources do not say. | What you searched and read. "Not found" is a finding; the script may say "the records do not say why". |
| **next video** | A neighbouring subject with a story of its own. Researched only as far as the core needs. | Its row in `next-videos.md`. |
| **off topic** | It does not bear on the main topic, or lies below the depth of a detail. Not researched. | Why, in a few words. |
| **open** | On topic, and the rounds ended before it was settled. | What would close it. It stays out of the video. |

Inside the main topic and down to the depth of a detail, "the core will not say that" does not
close a lead: researching it is how you learn whether the core needs it. Below that depth, the
lead closes as off topic ("below the detail depth"), however interesting. The edges of the
research are the topic and the depth, not the length of the video.

### Round 3: the double check

By now the notes are full and most of what is in them was read once, often quickly, sometimes
only in a search result. The third round spends its whole allowance on **being right**:

1. **List what the video will say.** Every fact, number, date, name, cause and quotation in the
   notes that is headed for the video (after a first script exists: `dump --claims`).
2. **Read each one again where it was first written.** Open the source and find the passage.
   Compare the number, the unit, the date, the spelling of the name and the words of the
   quotation with the note, character by character. A search summary, a snippet of a scan or
   your own memory does not count as reading it.
3. **Find a second, independent source** for anything that is surprising, anything the core
   says, every number that gives the size of the story, every quotation, and everything that
   reflects badly on a named person. Two reports that copy the same original are one source.
4. **Settle the contradictions** the notes contain: decide from the better source, or record
   that two sources differ, so that the video says both.
5. **Fix the labels.** Each claim ends as *Confirmed* (read at the source, and a second source
   where step 3 asks for one), *Single source* (the script names it), *Two sources differ* (the
   script gives both), or it leaves the list of things the video may say.
6. **Write the corrections into the notes**, marking the passage they correct, and count them
   in the index: checked, confirmed, corrected, differ, taken out.
7. **Once a script exists, compare it with the notes by machine**, and again after every
   revision. `node tools/dump.mjs <id> --quotes` lists each spoken quotation of four words or
   more that the notes do not hold word for word. `node tools/dump.mjs <id> --figures` lists
   each figure said or shown that is not in the notes as written. Neither uses a search. They
   find what steps 1 to 6 cannot: what changed between the note and the script (a quotation
   trimmed to fit, a figure rounded twice, digits swapped, a sum made while writing). Each item
   gets a verdict in `review/accuracy.md`. They do not show that a figure is on the right
   thing, or that the note is right; that is what the reading above is for.

Typical finds: a famous figure measures something else (one month, not one day; the group, not
the firm; an estimate made years later); a quotation was reported second-hand, or is a
paraphrase; a "because" in a summary has no support in the official account; a date is a day
out; two figures the notes added together overlap.

The check is better done by someone who did not write the note (section 9). The writer of a
note reads what they meant.

## 4. The questions to ask of every real event

When the video tells something that really happened, the notes must be able to answer all of
these. Each unanswered one is a lead, and on topic by definition: these are the questions of
round 1. What the answers mention in passing is tested like any other lead (section 3).

This table is the **depth of a detail**: each answer a sentence, a short list or a small table,
not a chapter. "The warnings" means who warned, when, and what was done, not the full text of
each memo. The core will use a few rows of it; the rest is there so you can choose them well.

| Ask | The notes hold |
| --- | --- |
| **Background** | What the world looked like before: the rule, the promise, the arrangement that the event broke, when it began and why. |
| **The people** | Each person by full name: their role on that day, their age if it matters, who they answered to, and what in their past explains what they did. Also the people usually left out: the second in command, the one who warned, the one who checked. |
| **The timeline** | A table in date order, and for the decisive days in clock time: what happened, who did it, the source for each row. Mark which times are disputed. |
| **The means** | How exactly it was done, step by step, with the amounts: which instrument, which account, which form, which rule was used or broken. If the video's subject plays a part, exactly which part (and which part it did *not* play). |
| **The rules at the time** | What was allowed, what was forbidden, what limits applied, who was supposed to check. |
| **The warnings** | Who saw it coming, when, what they wrote or said, and what was done about each warning. |
| **The numbers** | Every figure with unit, date, what it measures and who counted it (section 5). The growth of the key number over time, as a table that could become a chart. |
| **What was said** | Exact words of the people involved, on the day and afterwards, each traced to where it was first printed. |
| **The turning point** | The hour or the decision where it could have gone the other way, and what each side knew then. |
| **Who gained and who lost** | Each party, how much, and what kind of loss (money, a job, a reputation, a rule). Whether anyone outside the story was hurt. |
| **The findings** | What official inquiries, courts and later historians concluded, in their own words; who they blamed; what they could not establish. |
| **What came after** | The outcome for each person and each organisation in the story, in a fact or two each (not their later careers); what rules changed; what the event is called today and why. |
| **The disputes** | What is still argued about; the rival figures; the claims made by one side and denied by the other. |
| **The myths** | What is often said about it that is wrong or unproven. Search for the story as it is told in popular retellings, list each claim that the careful sources do not support, and keep the list under "Keep out of the script". |
| **The link to the subject** | Whether the main topic is at work in it, as the sources tell it (the example test, section 3), with the source; then why this event belongs in *this* video, and what it teaches that the invented example cannot. If the test fails, stop: the event is not an example for this video. |
| **For a beginner** | A plain explanation of each term and each step, and where that explanation simplifies. |

For a subject that is not an event (a mechanism, a method, a product), ask the same kind of
questions of it: who made it and when, how exactly it works step by step, what the numbers are,
where it fails, what is argued about, what people get wrong.

## 5. Numbers: back to where they were counted

A figure in a summary has usually been copied several times, and each copy drops a word. Follow
every figure that matters back to the body that counted it, and write down:

- **What it measures.** One day or a month? A closing price or the lowest price during the day?
  One office or the whole group? Before or after costs? The sum risked or the sum gained? A count
  at the time or an estimate made later?
- **Unit, currency and date.** And for money, whose money.
- **Who counted it**, and whether they had a reason to make it large or small.
- **The rival figures.** When sources differ, put them in a small table: figure, what it
  measures, who gives it. Often they do not disagree; they measure different things.

Then:

- **Never compare or chart two figures that measure different things.** A line chart of closing
  prices with one intraday low at the end is wrong even though every value is "from the source".
- **Never state a single-source figure flat.** Attribute it in the script, on screen as well as
  in the voice ("one newspaper put it at …").
- **Do sums yourself and show them.** A percentage, a multiple ("more than twice"), a total: mark
  it "worked out" and write the sum in the note.
- **Do not turn a figure into a comparison the source does not make.** "Three hundred times his
  limit" needs the limit and the holding to be in the same unit.

## 6. How to write a note

One note per topic. Name the file so the name alone says what is inside, numbered in reading
order: `01-how-long-is-a-year.md`, `02-the-leap-year-rule.md`. Use `research/_note-template.md`.
A deep note on one event can run to many hundreds of lines. That is expected: give it numbered
sections and a contents list, and keep going.

- **Start with `Summary:`** and a few sentences: what the note establishes, how sure it is, and
  what the video might use it for. Someone deciding whether to open the file reads only this.
- **Use headings that say what the section establishes** ("Why century years are skipped"), not
  topics ("Background"). The mind map points at headings (`02-the-leap-year-rule.md#Why century
  years are skipped`), so a heading is an address.
- **One claim per bullet or table row, with its source number** in square brackets (and the page
  or paragraph for a long document: `[1, 13.63]`), and a confidence word:
  - **Confirmed**: two independent sources agree, or one primary source says it.
  - **Single source**: one source that is not primary. Usable only with its attribution.
  - **Secondary only**: several accounts say it, none of them primary, and they may copy each
    other. Attribute it ("it is often reported that …") or leave it out.
  - **Disputed**: sources disagree, or one party asserts and another denies. The script gives
    both, or neither.
  - **Worked out**: you calculated it from sourced numbers. Show the sum.
  - **Search summary only**: you saw a search result's summary and could not open the page. A
    lead, not a source.
  - **General knowledge, unverified**: you believe it and did not find it. A lead, not a source.
  - **Unconfirmed**: you looked and could not verify it. Do not use in the video.
- **Keep fixed sections** at the end: "Everyday comparisons", "For a beginner" (plain
  explanations of the terms and steps, each with where it simplifies), "Other views and
  counter-examples", "Rival figures", "Keep out of the script" (myths and unsupported claims,
  each with why), "Could not confirm", "Sources".
- **Sources** are a numbered list: title, who published it, the link, and the date you read it.
  Say which you read in full, which in part, and which you could not open.
- **Quote exactly** when wording matters, in quotation marks, and keep quotes short. Otherwise
  restate in your own words. Never tidy a quotation.
- Do not paste whole pages. A note is what you concluded, with enough detail to check it.
- **When a later check proves a note wrong, correct the note**, and say in the note what was
  corrected and by which check. The script was written from the note; so will the next edition
  be.

Keep `research/index.md` up to date: one line per note (file, what it answers, how sure), the
count of leads in each round, and the table of the pass against the script (section 10).

## 7. Judging sources

Prefer, in this order:

1. **Primary sources**: the body that measures, defines or decides the thing (a statistics office,
   a standards body, an observatory, the law itself, the original paper, the product's own
   documentation). For an event: the official inquiry, the court's judgment, the institution's
   own record or bulletin, the minutes, a newspaper **of that day**, the words of people who were
   present.
2. **Reference works, textbooks and histories** written by people in the field.
3. **Careful secondary accounts**: established science and news publications, university pages.
4. **Everything else** (blogs, forums, video transcripts, answer sites, generated summaries): good
   for finding leads and for learning how beginners think; not a source for a claim.

Check each important claim:

- **Two independent sources.** Two pages that copy the same third page count as one. Look for
  where each got it.
- **The date.** Is this still true? Has the number been revised?
- **The exact wording.** Does the source say "causes" or "is linked with"? "All" or "most"? "Is"
  or "was, in one study"? "Found no evidence of" or "found that it did not happen"?
- **Who is speaking, and what do they gain?** A seller describing its own product is a source for
  what the product does, not for whether it is the best. A memoir is a source for what its author
  says happened.
- **Numbers you can re-derive, re-derive.** If the source says 97 days in 400 years and gives a
  yearly figure, multiply it out yourself.

Things that deserve extra care because they are often wrong: famous quotations (frequently
misattributed), "the first", "the largest", "a record", origin stories of words and customs,
round numbers, anything described as "studies show", and anything that makes a neat story.

## 8. Other views and counter-examples

A video that presents one side as if no other existed is fragile: a viewer who knows the objection
stops trusting everything else. Research the other side **as carefully as the main line**, with
its own note or its own section, and its own sources.

Look for:

- **The strongest serious objection** to the main claim, in the words of people who hold it.
- **Cases where the rule fails**: exceptions, edge cases, places or times where it was different.
- **Other ways of solving the same problem** (another country, another field, another era).
- **Popular stories that experts doubt.** A well-known legend is worth a note saying it is doubted.
- **Limits of the evidence**: small studies, old data, measurements that disagree.
- **What changed**: the earlier view, and why the field moved on.

Then decide, in the mind map, what the video does with each one:

- **Say it.** The default for the strongest objection and the commonest myth: two lines with
  their source, in the core. The others stay in the notes and the description.
- **Shape the wording** so the script stays true without discussing it ("we add a day", not "there
  is an extra day").
- **Leave it out**, marked `cut` in the mind map with its reason (it is false,
  it is unconfirmed, it would need an idea the viewer cannot be given), and mentioned in the
  video's description if a careful viewer might ask.

If you find nothing on the other side, write down what you searched for. "No serious objection
found after searching for X, Y and Z" is a finding.

**A real win needs a real loss, and its own risk.** If the video tells of someone who gained (a
famous bet, a company that grew, a cure that worked), research how it could have failed, and say
so in the story itself. Also research a real case that went the other way and show it next to
the first. One success told alone is an advertisement, whatever the warning two chapters earlier
says.

**What the requester remembers is a source, and it can be half right.** When the request
contains a fact ("if I remember correctly, in 1992 X used Y to do Z"), research it as its own
note and split it into its parts: who, when, by what means, with what result, who was hurt.
Give each part a verdict. Then:

- Tell the story **as the sources tell it**, not as it was remembered. Keep what the requester
  wanted from it (the event, the drama, the lesson).
- If the remembered link is wrong but the event is still worth telling, **say the difference
  aloud in the video** ("they did not use X; they used Y. But the idea was the same."). A
  viewer may carry the same half-memory.
- Put a small table in the note: remembered | what the sources say | in the video. Repeat its
  gist in the hand-over message, plainly and without fuss. Do not silently "fix" it, and do not
  repeat it unchecked because the requester said it.

## 9. Sharing the research between agents

Deep research on two or three events is long, and each event is independent. If you can start
other agents, give each one **one event or one topic** and let them run side by side. The agent
knows only what your brief says, so the brief carries the method, the edge and the end:

```text
Research <event> for a teaching video about <subject>. The viewer is <from the brief>.
The main topic, in one sentence: <the promise, and what this event shows about it>.
First, from the sources: does <the main topic> actually happen in this event (for example,
were <the thing> used, and how)? Write the answer and its source at the top of the note. If it
does not, stop there and report it: the event is not an example for this video.
Research to the depth of a detail: enough to tell the event with its people, decisive days,
exact means, amounts, warnings, findings and disputes, each in a sentence or a small table. Do
not go below it (later lives, documents in full, an institution's history). Everything off the
main topic is out of scope. A neighbouring subject with a story of its own: note what you met
under "Next-video candidates" (what, when, how it touches the main topic) and stop there.

Write one markdown note at <path>, following <path to _note-template.md>.
Two rounds, then stop:
1. The outline: answer every question in this list: <the table in section 4>.
2. The detail: re-read your note and list every person, organisation, place, date, number, term,
   mechanism, cause and quotation it mentions without explaining. Test each against the main
   topic: "could a sentence with the answer help this viewer understand the main topic?" Research
   those that pass, and the on-topic leads their answers raise, once. Write the others in the
   ledger as "off topic: why" and do not research them.
You may use at most <N> searches. If they run out, stop and say what is open.
Keep the ledger in the note: lead | found in | status (researched / not found: what you tried /
off topic: why / open: what would close it) | where the answer is. Report the counts per round.

Follow every number to the body that counted it; say what it measures; table the rival figures.
Open primary sources (<the ones stage 1 found>) rather than summaries. Mark each claim Confirmed,
Single source, Secondary only, Disputed, Worked out, Search summary only or Unconfirmed, and say
for each source how you read it: the page itself, a scan, or a snippet of a search result.
Quote exactly. Add nothing you did not find: no date, name, cause or figure from memory.
End the note with: For a beginner; Rival figures; Keep out of the script; Could not confirm;
Next-video candidates; Off-topic leads not followed; Sources (say which you read in full).
What is already known, to check and not to trust: <your notes so far, the requester's memory>.
```

When a note comes back: read all of it, not the agent's summary of it. Its findings are claims
like any others.

**The double check (round 3) goes to a different agent** from the one that wrote the note:

```text
Double-check a research note before a video is written from it. You did not write it.
The note: <path>. The main topic: <one sentence>. Do not research new subjects.

List every fact, number, date, name, cause and quotation in the note that a video could say.
Weakest first: those marked Search summary only, Unconfirmed, Secondary only or Single source;
places where the note contradicts itself; then the rest.
For each: open the source the note cites and read the passage. Compare number, unit, date, name
and quoted words exactly. For anything surprising, any number that gives the size of the story,
any quotation and anything that reflects badly on a named person, find a second source that does
not copy the first.
Append a section "Round 3: the double check (<date>)" with a table:
  no. | where in the note | the claim | confirmed / corrected / two sources differ / could not
  check | the source's words | source, and how you read it (page, scan, snippet)
and mark each passage of the note that the table corrects. Change nothing else.
You may use at most <N> searches; write results into the note as you go. If the allowance runs
out, stop and list what is unchecked. Do not get round the limit another way.
New questions you meet: list them under "Not followed". Do not research them.
```

**Plan the searches.** A session may allow a fixed number of web searches, shared by every
agent in it. Divide it before you start: about a third for round 1, a third for round 2, a
third kept for the double check. The two or three method searches of stage 1 come out of round
1's share first: a researcher short of searches tends to drop them for facts, and then has no
list of the field's common mistakes to test the script against. If the allowance is spent
before round 3, the facts in the video have been read once; that is the worse way to run out. A researcher that is told its
allowance is spent must stop, not look for another way to search.

**A snippet is not a page.** Text found by searching inside a scanned book locates a passage.
It is not a reading of the page: words may be missing and page numbers are approximate. Mark it
in the note; do not quote from it.

## 10. The pass against the script

The rounds check the notes. This pass checks **what the script did with them**, and it is done
when the script first passes `check`, and again after every edition. Open `research/index.md`
beside `node tools/dump.mjs <id> --text` and write a table in the index:

| The research has | The script | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| each real event, person, date, number, quotation, warning, finding and dispute in the notes | tells it / says it in a few words / does not use it | core · stays in the notes · next video · stays out (reason) |

**The question is what the core needs.** A researched fact goes into the core when the promise
needs it, when a real story cannot be told clearly without it (its people, date, means, amounts,
turning point, ending, link to the main topic), or when fairness needs it (a counter-view, a
dispute, how a win could have failed). Otherwise it **stays in the notes**: true and sourced,
material for the description, a longer version if the requester asks, or a next video. It
**stays out** of everything when:

- it is unconfirmed, or disputed in a way the video cannot state fairly;
- it needs an idea the viewer cannot be given at this level, even with a word card;
- it says the same thing as something already in the video;
- it does not bear on the main topic (the same test as an "off topic" lead, section 3).

Look for these losses in particular:

- **A real example told thin.** The note has forty facts about the event; the core has six
  lines and no date, no means and no link to the main topic. Short is fine; thin is not.
- **Every real example cut**, and the whole video told with invented people.
- **A fact shrank to a phrase.** The note has a paragraph on why something is so; the script has
  six words. If the fact goes against what a viewer would guess, six words are not enough
  (`teaching-techniques.md` section 5, "Say the viewer's guess").
- **A counter-view, a dispute or a myth with no place.**
- **A neighbouring story with no link.** The core mentions another event and never says what it
  shows about the main topic.
- **A person with a name and no role, or a role and no name.**

An independent checker (`script-review.md`, section 2) writes the same list from the other side:
"true and important in the note, but not in the script". Add what it finds.

## 11. When to stop

Stop when all of these are true:

- Rounds 1 and 2 are done: every lead in the ledger is researched, not found, off topic or open,
  and **the open ones are listed with what would close them**.
- Round 3 is done: every claim headed for the video has been read again at its source, the
  ones that need a second source have one, and the counts are in the index.
- For each real event, every question in section 4 has an answer or a "not found", at the depth
  of a detail and no deeper.
- Every neighbouring subject met is a row in `next-videos.md`, with what is known and what a
  video of its own would need.
- Every figure the video might use has been followed to who counted it, with what it measures.
- Every claim you expect to make is **confirmed** or honestly labelled, and each label says how
  the script must word it.
- You know the strongest objection, the disputes and the myths, and what the video does with
  each.
- You have at least one concrete example, one comparison, and one event that really happened,
  known well enough to tell briefly and correctly. If someone gains in it, you know how it
  could have failed, and you have one where someone lost.
- Anything the requester stated as a fact has a verdict.
- You can tell each story aloud, in order, with its dates, names and numbers, without the notes.

**Also stop when the rounds are used up**, even if questions remain: three rounds, and a fourth
only for named items the core needs. More searching is then the requester's decision. Say in the
index and in the hand-over where the research stopped and why (the rounds were done; an
allowance ran out; a source would not open), how many leads are open, how many were left as off
topic, which next videos were found, and what the video does not say because of it.

## 12. Mistakes to avoid

- **Stopping at the summary.** The first page of results gives the version everyone repeats.
  The detail and the corrections are in the inquiry report, the bulletin, the newspaper of the
  day.
- **Getting the depth wrong.** Research only what the core will say and you cannot choose it well
  or find a next video; research below a detail and you have done another video's work. One
  level down, then a row in `next-videos.md`.
- **Drifting, or going round again.** A lead tested against the lead that raised it, rather than
  against the main topic, leads away step by step; a fourth and fifth round of new questions
  feels thorough but the double check is what makes the video accurate. When the allowance is
  spent, stop and say so; do not work round it.
- **Citing a source for more than it says.** If you worked a figure out, mark it "worked out".
- **Presenting a search summary as a source you read.**
- **Losing the link between a claim and its source.** Add the source number as you write the
  bullet, not afterwards.
- **Inventing details to make a story complete** (a date, a name, a quotation). If the research
  does not have it, the video does not say it.
- **Adding a cause the source does not give.** The report says the markets lost nothing; it does
  not say why. Then the video does not say why either. A neat "because" is the easiest thing to
  invent without noticing.
- **Dropping the hedge.** "As far as the inquiry could tell", "is reported to have said", "one
  account says": when the source hedges, the script hedges, in the voice and on screen.
- **Mixing measures.** Closing prices with a low of the day; one firm's figure with the group's;
  a day's total with a month's.
- **Treating research as finished when the script starts.** Writing exposes new questions; they
  are leads. Research them and add to the notes.
- **Fixing the script and not the note.** The same error returns in the next edition.
- **Writing new lines in the words of the source.** A line written straight from a research
  note brings the note's vocabulary with it ("traders", "grain", "hedge"). Write it in the
  words the video has already taught, or teach the word first.
- **Stretching a figure when making it simple.** "The loss was more than twice the bank's
  capital" must not become "more than all the money the bank had". Simplify the wording, not
  the claim; then check the simple sentence against the source again.
